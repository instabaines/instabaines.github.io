Title: The CEO Algorithm: Why Corporate Cruelty is a Design Feature, Not a Bug
Date: 2026-07-12
Summary: CEOs don't make cruel decisions because they are uniquely evil. They make them because they are trapped inside an optimization algorithm that mandates cruelty as a metric of success.

When a mega-corporation announces a massive wave of layoffs while reporting record profits and approving a $50 million CEO compensation package, the public reaction is predictable. Headlines flare with moral outrage. We look at the decision-makers and wonder: *How can these people sleep at night?*

The problem isn't a lack of personal morality. Diagnosing it that way lets the real culprit off the hook.

The reality of modern corporate capitalism is more mechanical: **CEOs don't make cruel decisions because they are uniquely evil; they make them because they are trapped inside an optimization algorithm that mandates cruelty as a metric of success.**

## The Single Objective Function

In software engineering, an optimization algorithm is given a single, unyielding objective function. It will eliminate any variable that obstructs that goal.

In publicly traded corporations, that objective function is **the maximization of shareholder value.**

A CEO is not an absolute monarch. Legally, they are an employee of the shareholders — the institutional funds, hedge funds, and everyday investors who own the stock. Under corporate law, the CEO owes a fiduciary duty to those owners. Employees, customers, and local communities carry no weight in that legal obligation.

If a CEO prioritizes human empathy — say, by keeping 10,000 workers on payroll during a downturn — the algorithm triggers a correction. Margins dip. The stock stalls. An activist hedge fund buys up shares, kicks the door down, fires the empathetic CEO, and installs someone who cuts the jobs anyway.

The system doesn't reward kindness. It filters it out.

## Capital Rules, Labor Pays

Executive compensation hyper-optimizes this trap. Regular employees trade labor for a taxed paycheck. CEOs trade structural decisions for capital — large packages of stock options.

When a CEO cuts headcount, markets typically applaud and the share price rises. Because the CEO's net worth is tied directly to that price, a decision that destroys thousands of livelihoods instantly manufactures tens of millions of dollars for the person who signed the pink slips.

The executive's personal wealth is deliberately aligned with the investor's profit and completely decoupled from the workforce's well-being. That is not a side effect. It is the design.

## The Danger of the "Evil CEO" Narrative

Why does it matter whether we blame the person or the system? Because focusing on individual greed keeps us running on a treadmill of useless outrage.

If mass layoffs are isolated moral failures of "bad people," the solution is simply to wait for "good people" to take over — the mythical ethical billionaire who will fix it from the inside. But they aren't coming. And if they did, the market would break or replace them within a fiscal quarter.

Explaining the mechanics is not the same as justifying them. Acknowledging that an algorithm works exactly as designed doesn't make the output less devastating for the families it affects.

But if we want to change the output, we have to stop pleading with the machine to be nicer. We have to change the code. Until labor laws, tax policy, and corporate governance are rewritten to count human cost as a liability, the machine will keep doing exactly what it was built to do.
