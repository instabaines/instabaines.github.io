Title: Engineering Normal: How Corporations Manufacture the Things We "Need"
Date: 2026-05-01
Summary: From deodorant to oat milk, the things we consider basic needs were deliberately engineered by corporations — using shame, cultural imprinting, and manufactured demand to turn natural human states into problems only they could solve.

Think about the things you consider basic requirements for existing in polite society. You wake up, brush your teeth, rinse with mouthwash, apply deodorant, and spray a bit of cologne or perfume. On your way to work, you might grab a latte with oat milk.

We view these choices as matters of personal hygiene, taste, and lifestyle. But if you look closely at history, you realize a fascinating truth: **Many of our everyday "needs" were meticulously engineered by corporations over the last century.**

Before an industry can sell a solution, it often has to manufacture the problem.

---

## The Pet Paradox: Why Are Humans the Only "Pungent" Animals?

To understand how deeply we have been conditioned, consider a quick psychological comparison. Most of us live closely with pets. We tolerate "wet dog smell" or the distinct scent of a cat without a second thought. We accept it because it is natural; a dog smelling like a dog is neutral.

Yet, if a human being smells like a human being, it is treated as a social emergency.

Humans have carried natural body scents for millennia without it being an obstacle to socialization. So, how did the natural state of our biology become a social failure? The answer lies in a century of **demand engineering.**

---

## 1. Pathologizing the Baseline: The Invention of Social Shame

In the early 20th century, products like deodorant and mouthwash were failing luxuries. People simply didn't think they needed them. To grow the market, advertisers had to alter the social signal.

### Odorono (1919) and the "Unseemly" Body

In 1919, a deodorant company called Odorono was struggling because people viewed blocking sweat as unhealthy. Legendary copywriter **James Webb Young** turned the campaign around at the J. Walter Thompson agency by running an ad in *Ladies' Home Journal* titled *"Within the Curve of a Woman’s Arm."*

The ad didn't praise the product; it attacked the consumer's social standing, poetically suggesting that women who seemed perfectly fine were secretly disgusting their friends with underarm odor. The ad caused controversy—many women canceled their magazine subscriptions—but Odorono's sales rose **112%** within a year. An industry was born out of engineered insecurity.

1. Isolate a Natural State ──> 2. Rebrand it as a Social Failure ──> 3. Sell the Cure

### Listerine (1920s) and "Halitosis"

A few years later, Lambert Pharmacal Company pulled off an identical feat with **Listerine**. Originally sold as a floor cleaner and surgical antiseptic, it wasn’t selling well as a mouth rinse.

In the 1920s, copywriters Milton Feasley and Gordon Seagrove dug up an obscure, Latin-based medical term from an old dictionary: **Halitosis** (meaning bad breath). They launched a wildly successful campaign featuring "Edna"—a beautiful, talented woman who remained "often a bridesmaid, but never a bride" because of her breath. By medicalizing a normal human trait, Listerine's profits skyrocketed from around **$100,000 in 1921 to over $4 million by 1927**.

## 2. The Long Game: Nestlé’s "Coffee Candy" Strategy (1975)

Sometimes corporations don’t exploit an existing fear; they implant entirely new cultural codes. This is exactly how **Nestlé** turned Japan—a nation with thousands of years of deep-rooted tea culture—into one of the largest coffee consumers on earth.

In the 1970s, Nestlé’s traditional marketing for instant coffee was a complete failure in Japan. While consumers liked the taste in blind tests, they wouldn't buy it. The company brought in French psychoanalyst and marketing consultant **Dr. Clotaire Rapaille**, who discovered the root cause: **Japanese consumers had zero childhood memories associated with coffee.** It carried no emotional signal.

> *"Abandon all efforts to market coffee to adults,"* Rapaille advised Nestlé.

Instead, Nestlé shifted its focus to children. They flooded the Japanese market with sweet, coffee-flavored candies and treats. They weren't looking for immediate coffee sales; they were playing a **10-to-15-year long game**.

By the time that generation reached adulthood, the flavor profile of coffee was no longer foreign. It was deeply "imprinted" alongside memories of childhood sweetness. Today, Japan is the sixth-largest importer of coffee in the world.

## 3. The Modern Playbook: Oatly and "Milk Shame" (2016)

Lest we think this is ancient history, the exact same signal manipulation happened just a few years ago in the beverage aisle.

For decades, cow's milk was heralded as "Nature’s perfect food" via heavily funded dairy campaigns. When the Swedish food-tech company **Oatly** entered the U.S. market in 2016 under CEO Toni Petersson, they didn't try to win over everyday supermarket shoppers immediately. Instead, they used a **barista-led, foodservice-first strategy**.

Oatly bypassed traditional shelves and went directly to high-end, independent coffee shops in New York City, introducing their "Barista Edition." By positioning oat milk as a premium choice that frothed better for high-quality lattes, they allowed baristas to act as cultural gatekeepers.

They successfully flipped the signal: ordering a coffee with regular dairy milk was subtly rebranded as unrefined, heavy, or outdated. Oatly manufactured a new cultural norm, leading to a massive structural shift in global plant-based dairy consumption and a multi-billion dollar IPO in 2021.

## The Calibrated Consumer

We like to think of our purchases as rational expressions of our free will. But history reveals that our environment is continuously being calibrated.

* The perfume industry convinced us that human skin is naturally "pungent."
* The dental aisle convinced us that natural breath is a medical condition called "halitosis."
* Nestlé convinced a tea-drinking nation that coffee tastes like home.
* Modern alt-dairy brands convinced us that cow's milk is an unrefined choice.

We are the only species on Earth that pays a premium just to alter our baseline biological selves to fit a corporate-engineered standard of "normal."
